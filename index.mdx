A chip case looks simple until six people sit down and someone asks how many of each colour they are meant to have. These notes cover the practical side of running chips at a home game night: knowing what is in the case, counting it without fuss, and handing out stacks that do not need constant change-making. Everything here is about logistics. Chips in these pages are scoring tokens worth points, nothing more.

## Why chip logistics deserve a plan

Three logistics problems come up at almost every casual table.

**The set does not match the guest list.** A common retail case holds 300 or 500 chips spread over four or five colours. The split is rarely even, and the colour you need most is often the scarcest.

**Nobody agrees what a colour is worth.** Most home sets follow a familiar ladder: white for 1 point, red for 5, green for 25, black for 100, and sometimes blue for 10. The [chip colour and value reference](https://pokerchip.live/poker-chip-values) lists the common colours and lets you build a stack to see its total. Whatever values you pick, say them aloud before the first hand.

**Change-making stalls the game.** If the gap between two colours is too wide, players keep breaking large chips for small ones. Every break is a pause, and pauses add up across an evening.

## The three-step routine

1. **Count** what you own, by colour, and write the totals down once.
2. **Plan** a starting stack from the number of seats, not from the case. Decide the stack first, then check the case can cover it.
3. **Hand out** stacks in identical, pre-counted piles so every seat starts level and the first hand begins on time.

If you would rather skip the physical count altogether, [PokerChip.live](https://pokerchip.live/) runs digital chips on each player's phone in the browser. The host opens a room, shares a code, and stacks are tracked for everyone at once.
